Flour weight correction vs. ambient humidity

Based on published wheat/flour moisture sorption isotherm data (ASABE D245.5, BAKERpedia, milling standards)

How to read this: Recipes specify flour by weight, but a gram of flour is never 100% flour solids — some of that gram is water. Flour's moisture content rises and falls with the equilibrium relative humidity (ERH) of its storage environment (it slowly adsorbs or releases water until it matches the surrounding air, following a sigmoid "moisture sorption isotherm"). If your flour is more humid than the recipe writer's flour, each measured gram contains less actual flour solids — so you're effectively under-flouring relative to their intent (and vice versa for drier flour).

The correction factor here answers: "if I want the same mass of dry flour solids the recipe intended, how much should I actually weigh out?" It is calculated as:

corrected grams = recipe grams × (1 − baseline moisture) / (1 − your flour's moisture)

On the baseline (the number that shifts this whole curve): there is no recorded standard, because recipe writers essentially never measure or state their flour's moisture. Common reference points: 14% is the milling trade's accounting basis for quoting protein/ash, not a typical measured value; 13% is the standardization point used in industrial baking test protocols; 12% is a reasonable estimate for retail flour that has equilibrated to ordinary indoor conditions (~50% RH), and is the default here; and directly-measured bagged flour has come in as low as ~10%. Note that a 14% baseline sits near the practical ceiling (flour much wetter risks spoilage), so it makes the correction almost always negative — pick it only if you think the recipe's author worked in a genuinely humid kitchen.

Caveats that matter more than the math: (1) Flour doesn't teleport to equilibrium — a bag sealed in a cupboard may sit for months at whatever moisture it had when milled/packaged, largely decoupled from today's weather; only long-term storage in a humidity-permeable container really tracks ambient RH. (2) The isotherm itself shifts a couple percentage points with temperature and with flour type (whole wheat holds slightly more water than white; there's also adsorption/desorption hysteresis). (3) In practice, most bakers correct hydration by feel — adjusting the water rather than the flour weight — which sidesteps needing to know moisture content precisely at all. (4) Outdoor RH from a weather lookup is a proxy, not your pantry — indoor heating/AC and closed containers can put your flour's real environment well off from what's reported outside. This chart is most useful as a sense of magnitude: typically a 1–3% swing, occasionally larger in extreme climates, rather than a precision instrument.
